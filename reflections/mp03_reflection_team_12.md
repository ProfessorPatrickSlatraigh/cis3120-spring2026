# MP03 Comparative Reflection — Team 12
CIS 3120 — Programming for Analytics · Baruch College

The geographic patterns surfaced by this analysis reveal a fundamental asymmetry in how
Financial Services and Travel & Hospitality firms deploy and consolidate physical capacity —
an asymmetry that mirrors the underlying economics of each industry.

Financial Services location events cluster in two distinct geographies: major financial
centers (New York, Charlotte, Chicago) for capital-markets and corporate-office activity,
and Sun Belt metros (Phoenix, Dallas, Atlanta) for operations-center relocations and branch
consolidations. The Sun Belt pattern reflects a decade-long cost rationalization trend in
banking: routine processing work migrates to lower-cost labor markets while customer-facing
branches in high-cost urban cores are consolidated. The dominant event types — closures,
consolidations, and relocations — confirm the sector is in a fixed-cost reduction phase.
This is consistent with the post-2022 rise in interest rates, which has pressured net
interest margins and accelerated branch rationalization across both money-center and regional
banks. Capital is not being deployed into new physical footprint; it is being extracted from
excess footprint.

Travel & Hospitality events tell the opposite story. Markers concentrate in leisure
destinations (Las Vegas, Orlando, Miami, Hawaii) and in international gateway airports, with
the dominant event types being openings, expansions, and route launches. This pattern
reflects demand-driven expansion: the post-pandemic recovery in leisure travel has continued
to outpace pre-pandemic trends, and hotel operators and airlines have responded by committing
capital to new properties and routes where occupancy and load-factor data justify the
investment. The geographic contrast is stark — Travel & Hospitality's physical footprint is
growing in exactly the markets where Financial Services is shrinking its own.

This asymmetry has a structural interpretation. Financial Services firms hold fixed-cost-heavy
balance sheets where surplus real estate is pure drag; the rational response to margin
pressure is exit. Travel & Hospitality firms hold asset-light franchise structures (Marriott,
Hilton) or operate in markets with high barriers to new entry (slot-constrained airports,
resort destinations), so physical presence is a competitive moat worth investing in. The map,
in short, visualizes the difference between an industry optimizing its cost base and an
industry competing for scarce capacity. A limitation of this analysis is that the 8-K corpus
skews toward U.S. events; the international expansion of T&H operators — arguably the larger
story — is underrepresented because non-U.S. city geocoding was excluded from scope.
